#+TITLE: SEC Filing Cross-Reference Analysis Project Summary
#+AUTHOR: Research Team
#+DATE: 2026-01-20

* Original Research Intent

** Goal
Identify instances where firms provide information about other firms that conflicts with or adds to the mentioned firm's own disclosures - essentially finding "competing narratives" in SEC filings.

** Motivating Example
From prior analysis: AMD's 10-K describes Intel as "dominant" in the processor market, while Intel's own 10-K states they have "lost market share." This contradiction reveals how competitive positioning shapes disclosure narratives.

** Research Question
Can we systematically extract cross-company references from 10-K filings to find:
1. Conflicting narratives (Firm A says X about Firm B; Firm B says Y about itself)
2. Information that adds to what the mentioned firm discloses
3. Substantive intelligence not available from the mentioned firm's own filings

* Methodology

** Phase 1: Scale Up the Sample
- Downloaded SEC EDGAR master indexes for Q1 2023, 2024, 2025
- Randomly sampled *480 10-K filings* across 3 fiscal years (FY2022-2024)
- Total corpus: *7.5 GB* of filings from diverse industries

** Phase 2: Initial Entity Extraction
- Ran the existing =scr.entity= extraction pipeline with =sec-filers-only= filtering
- Matched extracted company names against the SEC filer registry (CIK resolution)
- Classified relationship types (supplier, customer, competitor, partner, etc.)

* Key Findings

** Finding 1: Most Cross-Company Mentions Are Not Substantive

The vast majority (~80%) of cross-company mentions in 10-Ks fall into low-value categories:

| Category                   | Example                                      | Value |
|----------------------------+----------------------------------------------+-------|
| Risk factor lists          | "We compete with Amazon, Apple, Microsoft…" | Low   |
| Executive bios             | "Ms. Smith worked at Google from 2015-2020" | None  |
| Tenant lists (REITs)       | "Properties include Apple Store, Tesla…"    | Low   |
| Generic competitive landscape | "Industry participants include Intel, AMD…" | Low   |

** Finding 2: The Information Asymmetry Problem

SEC disclosure requirements create a structural limitation:

#+BEGIN_QUOTE
Filer must disclose → Filer's own risks, dependencies, vulnerabilities
Filer is NOT required to disclose → Strategic information about other firms
#+END_QUOTE

Most disclosures take the form: "/We/ depend on Entity X" or "Entity X is /our/ largest customer"

This reveals information *about the filer*, not about the mentioned entity. The mentioned entity:
- Already knows its own supplier/customer relationships
- Already knows the contract terms (they signed them)
- Is not surprised by any of this information

** Finding 4: Competing Narratives Are Extremely Rare

*The AMD-Intel example from the original sample remains the only clear instance of competing narratives.*

Why competing narratives are rare:
1. Companies avoid directly naming competitors in ways that could be challenged
2. Disclosure language is vetted by lawyers to be defensible
3. Quantitative claims are typically about the filer's own metrics
4. Characterizations of other firms use hedging language

The random 480-filing sample found *zero bidirectional relationships* where both companies mention each other, compared to 8 in the original curated 92-filing sample. This suggests:
- The original sample was curated to include known competitors (AMD-Intel, Ford-GM, Delta-American, etc.)
- Random sampling rarely captures both sides of a competitive relationship
- Competing narratives require both firms to discuss each other substantively

** Finding 5: The Real Value May Be in Aggregation

Individual cross-references are rarely valuable for understanding the mentioned firm. However, *aggregating across many filers* could reveal:

1. *Market power indicators*: If 50 suppliers all list Company X as a major customer with concentration risk, X has significant buyer power
2. *Supply chain mapping*: Aggregating "who supplies whom" creates network graphs
3. *Dependency patterns*: Identifying which firms are mentioned as "sole source" or "critical supplier"

* Conclusions

** What We Learned

1. *Competing narratives are rare* - The AMD-Intel example is exceptional, not representative. Most cross-company mentions are legally cautious and non-controversial.

2. *Information asymmetry flows the wrong direction* - Disclosures reveal the /filer's/ dependence, not the /mentioned firm's/ strategy. The mentioned firm already knows its own relationships.

3. *Substantive value is concentrated* - Only ~15-20% of mentions contain actionable data. Customer concentration percentages are the most valuable category.

4. *Random sampling doesn't find bidirectional relationships* - Finding competing narratives requires targeting known competitors or industry clusters.

5. *Aggregation may be more valuable than individual extraction* - The real insight might come from asking "How many firms list Apple as a 10%+ customer?" rather than "What does Firm X say about Apple?"

** Limitations of the Approach

- The mentioned firm already knows what its suppliers/customers disclose about it
- Truly conflicting information would create legal liability, so it's avoided
- Qualitative characterizations (like AMD calling Intel "dominant") are rare and subjective
- Bidirectional analysis requires curated samples of known business relationships

** Potential Paths Forward

1. *Curated competitor analysis*: Instead of random sampling, download 10-Ks for known competitor pairs (AMD-Intel, Ford-GM, Uber-Lyft, etc.) and specifically compare their characterizations of each other and the market

2. *Supply chain aggregation*: Build a database of "who mentions whom as a major customer" to map supply chain dependencies that aren't visible from any single filing

3. *Temporal analysis*: Track how the /same/ cross-reference changes over time (e.g., does a customer's share of revenue grow or shrink?)

4. *Event-driven analysis*: Focus on 8-K filings around specific events (M&A announcements, contract terminations) where competing narratives are more likely
